A root canal is one of the most misunderstood costs in dentistry, because the
number depends heavily on which tooth is being treated and who is treating it.
Here is how to think about it clearly.

## Why the price varies so much

A root canal removes infected or inflamed pulp from inside a tooth, disinfects
the canals, and seals the tooth. The work involved depends on the tooth:

- **Front teeth** typically have one canal and are the least complex.
- **Premolars** usually have one or two canals.
- **Molars** can have three or four canals, and much more of the work happens
  out of direct view.

More canals and a more complex root shape mean more time and skill, which is the
main reason a molar costs more than a front tooth.

## What the fee usually includes — and what it does not

The root canal fee generally covers cleaning and sealing the inside of the
tooth. It usually does **not** include:

- The **permanent restoration** the tooth needs afterward. A back tooth will
  typically need a crown to protect it from fracturing; a front tooth may only
  need a filling.
- Any **build-up** needed if too much tooth structure is gone.
- A **retreatment** if the original treatment fails years later.

So "the cost of a root canal" is really the cost of the root canal plus the
restoration. Always ask for the total plan, not just the first line item.

## What insurance does

Most dental plans that cover endodontic treatment cover it as a major or basic
service, often at around half of the allowed fee, subject to your annual maximum
and any deductible. Plans vary widely, and some have waiting periods for major
work. Your insurer can give you the specific allowed amount for your tooth type
and provider.

## Questions to ask before you commit

- What is the total cost, including the final restoration?
- Is the treating dentist a general dentist or an endodontist, and does that
  change my out-of-pocket?
- What does my insurance pay, and what is my patient portion?
- Is there a payment plan or financing option?
- What happens if I choose extraction instead, and what does replacing the
  tooth cost over time?

## The comparison that matters

It is tempting to compare the root canal fee to the cost of an extraction.
Extraction is usually cheaper on the day. But a removed tooth often needs to be
replaced — with an implant or bridge — and that cost can exceed the root canal
and crown combined. Keeping a tooth that can be saved is frequently the better
long-term value.

Ask for a written estimate and a clear explanation of each option. That is the
only way to compare fairly.
