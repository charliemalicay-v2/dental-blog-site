Most people grow up hearing "every six months." It is a reasonable default, and
for many healthy adults it works well. But six months is a starting point, not a
rule handed down from on high. Dentists adjust the interval based on what is
actually happening in your mouth.

## Where the six-month figure comes from

The twice-a-year cadence is a long-standing convention that sets a predictable
rhythm for catching problems while they are small. The American Dental
Association frames checkups as a way to detect disease early, when treatment
tends to be simpler and less expensive. It does not present six months as a
universal requirement — it presents regular, individualized care as the goal.

## What actually changes your interval

A dentist recommending a schedule is weighing several variables:

- **Gum health.** A history of gum disease, deep pockets, or bleeding tends to
  shorten the interval, often to three or four months.
- **Cavity history.** If you have had several restorations, you are more likely
  to develop more, so monitoring matters more.
- **Dry mouth.** Many medications reduce saliva, and saliva is a natural
  protective buffer. Less saliva often means more frequent visits.
- **Smoking or vaping.** These raise the risk of gum disease and can mask its
  early signs.
- **Pregnancy.** Hormonal changes can affect gum tissue, so some patients need
  closer monitoring for a period.
- **Home care and diet.** Consistent brushing, flossing, and limited
  sugar exposure can support a longer interval.

## What happens during a routine visit

A typical preventive visit is two things happening together: an exam and a
cleaning.

The exam looks for decay, cracks, worn restorations, gum inflammation, and
changes in soft tissue. Depending on your risk and history, the dentist may take
X-rays. The cleaning removes plaque and hardened tartar that a toothbrush cannot
reach, then polishes the surfaces.

## The honest answer

For a healthy adult with no gum disease and a good home routine, every six
months is often fine. If you have gum disease, a dry mouth, a high cavity rate,
or you smoke, a shorter interval is frequently the better choice. The right
answer comes from an exam, not a calendar.

If it has been a while since your last visit, that is a normal place to start.
The team can look, tell you what they see, and set an interval that fits your
actual risk.
